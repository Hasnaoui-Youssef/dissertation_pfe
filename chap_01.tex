\section*{Introduction}
This chapter states the setting of the project and the problem it addresses. It presents the host organization and the origin of the subject, establishes what an execution record supplies that the ordinary means of debugging do not, states the question the project answers, surveys what the devices provide and what the available tools expose of it, and closes with the objectives, the constraints and the deliverables.

\section{Host Organization}

This section presents the organization within which the project was carried out. It introduces STMicroelectronics, then the Tunis site that hosted the work, and then the team whose activity gave rise to the subject.

\subsection{STMicroelectronics}

STMicroelectronics (ST) is a global semiconductor manufacturer, founded in 1987 through the merger of SGS Microelettronica of Italy and Thomson Semiconducteurs of France. It supplies devices for the automotive, industrial, personal electronics and communications markets \cite{st_comp}.

Among its product lines is the STM32 family of microcontrollers, built on the ARM Cortex-M processors. That family is the subject of this work, and Section \ref{sec:sota} surveys its trace capability.

\subsection{ST Tunis}

The STMicroelectronics Tunis site, located in the El Ghazela Technopark, employs over 100 professionals in fields such as tools, applications, quality and support functions. The site contributes to circuit design, software tool development, application support, and the electrical validation of new circuits.

\subsection{Application \& Product Support Team}

The Application \& Product Support team provides technical support for ST's tools and products, and manages and publishes the STM32 product documentation. It answers customer questions on the use of the devices, maintains the accuracy of the documents that describe them, and produces application notes and training material stating how a given capability is used in practice.

The subject of this project originates from concrete needs identified within the team and STMicroelectronics. In the course of its activities, the team routinely handles customer issues of varying complexity, constraints and contexts. While the majority of these problems can be resolved through conventional debugging means, the limited utilization of instruction trace capabilities has been identified as a potential factor slowing down the resolution and root-cause analysis of certain issues. Moreover, access to this feature is currently being tied to specific hardware and proprietary tools. This situation has given rise to a growing demand for an independent instruction trace implementation, free of hardware and vendor-specific dependencies, a request expressed by customers and one that remained as a backlog item since 2017. It is in response to this long-standing need that the present project was conceived.

\section{Observing an Executing Program}
\label{sec:observability}

A defect is a condition in a program that causes its behavior to violate its specification. To locate a defect, the developer must find the point at which the program departs from its specification, and finding that point requires observing how the program executed. Consider a program required to toggle a LED once per second. It may fail because a clock is not configured, or because it writes an incorrect register. These are two distinct defects that produce an identical symptom, so the symptom alone cannot separate them. Separating them requires observing the intermediate behavior of the program.

The techniques that supply this visibility are distinguished along three axes, and each technique occupies a position on every axis. Table \ref{tab:debug_axes} states the three axes that concern this work.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Axes of debugging technique}
    \label{tab:debug_axes}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{| @{}>{\hsize=0.75\hsize\bfseries}Y | >{\hsize=1.25\hsize}Y@{} |}
        \hline
        Axis & \textbf{Distinction} \\
        \hline
        Intrusive and non-intrusive & Whether applying the technique alters the behavior of the program under examination. \\
        \hline
        Live and post-mortem & Whether the program is examined under the control of a debugger or from a record produced during execution and analyzed afterward. \\
        \hline
        Run-control and trace-based & Whether the developer directs the investigation by choosing where to stop, or reconstructs it from a recording of the program flow. \\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

This section examines three ways of observing a program and places each on the three axes above. The first is halting the processor. The developer stops the program at chosen points and reads its state. This technique is live and run-control based, and it is intrusive, because stopping the processor alters the timing of everything around it. The second is instrumenting the program, by adding code that makes the program report on itself as it runs. This technique is live and trace-based, and it too is intrusive, because the added code and the cost of transmitting its output alter how the program runs. The third is instruction trace. Hardware records the flow of the program as it executes, and the host reads that record once the program has stopped. This technique is trace-based and post-mortem, and it is non-intrusive, because the recording is done by dedicated hardware and adds no code to the program. The section then examines what each technique reveals, and establishes that one class of defect can be reached only by instruction trace.

\subsection{State at a Halt}

When the processor is halted, the host may read the register file, the contents of memory, the registers of the peripherals, and the registers of the debug components themselves. From the stack pointer and the debug information held in the program image, an unwinder reconstructs the frames that are still live.

Each of these is a view of the state the device holds at the instant of the halt. The state at a given instant describes the values the program holds at that instant. It carries no record of the path the program took to reach it. The order in which the instructions ran, the branches the program took, the calls that have already returned, and the handlers that have already completed are gone by the time the processor stops, and a later read cannot recover them. Where the stack itself has been damaged, the unwinder cannot proceed, and the live frames become unavailable as well.

Halting the processor is also less passive than it appears. The processor stops, but the rest of the system keeps running. Timers continue to count, transfers already programmed into a direct memory access controller continue to run, and a peer on a communication link observes a device that has stopped responding. Some devices provide controls that freeze selected peripherals while the processor is halted. Whatever lies outside the device cannot be frozen by these controls.

\subsection{The Cost of Instrumentation}

The alternative to halting is to instrument the program, so that it reports on itself as it runs. Transmitting that report has a cost, and that cost falls on the system under examination.

Consider a character sent over a UART configured for 115\,200 baud, with eight data bits, no parity, and one stop bit. That character occupies ten bit periods, which is 86.8\,\textmu s on the wire. On a processor clocked at 600\,MHz, those 86.8\,\textmu s are roughly 52\,000 cycles for a single character, and a blocking implementation spends all of them waiting.

Moving the transmission to a direct memory access controller removes the stall, but it introduces a perturbation of its own. The controller becomes a second master on the bus matrix, and it contends for the same memory banks and the same slave ports as the instruction and data ports of the processor. It also raises an interrupt on completion. A timing-dependent defect is most sensitive to two factors, the arbitration on the interconnect and the interrupt load of the system, and instrumenting the program alters both.

\subsection{Defects Requiring Execution History}

Some defects lie beyond the reach of both techniques. Table \ref{tab:defect_classes} states the principal cases. For each case, it gives the evidence available once the processor has stopped, and the evidence that a record of the execution supplies.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Defect classes and the evidence available to investigate them}
    \label{tab:defect_classes}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{| @{}>{\hsize=1.0\hsize\bfseries}Y | >{\hsize=1.0\hsize}Y | >{\hsize=1.0\hsize}Y@{} |}
        \hline
        Defect & \textbf{Available once halted} & \textbf{Supplied by an execution record} \\
        \hline
        Memory corrupted by a stray write & The corrupted contents, and the address at which the program eventually faults & The instructions that ran before the corruption, among them the store that performed it \\
        \hline

        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

All of these cases share a single requirement. To investigate them, the developer must know the order in which the instructions executed, alongside the state the program held at the halt. They therefore call for a technique that is non-intrusive, that permits post-mortem examination, and that is trace-based. Of the three techniques, only instruction trace holds all three of these positions.

The same record serves purposes beyond locating defects. Because it records which instructions executed and how often, it yields code coverage without instrumenting the program, and it identifies the paths on which execution time is spent.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Such a record is produced by hardware. Much of the STM32 range carries a trace unit that reports the flow of the program as it executes, and part of the range carries a buffer into which that report is captured without leaving the device. The hardware alone does not make the record usable. It must be configured, the capture must be retrieved, and the result must be interpreted against the program image before it means anything to a developer. Each of those steps requires software, and outside the environment of a tool vendor that software does not exist.

The question this project answers is the following. How are the instruction trace capabilities of STM32 devices made reachable within an open and integrated debug chain, without a dedicated trace probe and without dependence on a proprietary environment?

The question divides into four. Configuration asks how a host configures the trace components of a device over the ordinary debug connection, when their presence, their placement and their addresses are properties of the particular device, which the architecture leaves unspecified. Capture asks how the record is retained and retrieved when no trace pins and no capture hardware are available. Interpretation asks how a capture is turned back into the instructions that executed, and whether that is done independently of the session that produced it. Restitution asks how the result is presented in source terms, inside a development environment the developer already uses. The chapters that follow answer them in turn.

\section{State of the Art: Instruction Trace on ARM Microcontrollers}
\label{sec:sota}

The survey below is restricted to instruction trace on ARM M-profile microcontrollers. Conventional debugging on these devices is not surveyed, since breakpoints, memory inspection and single stepping are supported uniformly by every tool considered here and raise no question this project needed to answer. Three bodies of work bear on instruction trace specifically: what the devices provide, what the available tools expose of it, and what published work has attempted with it.

\subsection{Trace Capability of the STM32 Devices}
\label{subsec:sota_devices}

Whether instruction trace is available at all is a property of the device. A family implements a trace unit or it does not, and independently of that it carries a buffer able to hold the captured trace inside the device or it does not. Where no such buffer exists the trace leaves only through dedicated pins, which requires those pins to be routed on the board and hardware able to capture them.

\subsection{Proprietary Development Environments}
\label{subsec:sota_proprietary}

Instruction trace on ARM microcontrollers is exposed principally by the development environments of IAR, Keil and SEGGER. Each couples a debugger to a debug probe of its own make, and reaching the trace means working inside that environment with that probe \cite{iar_etm_trace}.

These environments expose a fixed set of tracing settings through their interface, and reapply that configuration at every resume. The behavior is consistent from the point of view of the tool, and it leaves a developer no means of holding a configuration of their own.

The probes impose limitations of their own. IAR, Keil and SEGGER each supply a base model and an extended model, the latter capturing trace through dedicated pins. Table \ref{tab:debug_probe_limitations} states which environment accepts which probe.

\subsection{Open-Source Debug Software}
\label{subsec:sota_open}

The open-source tools divide the problem differently and stop at a different point in it.

OpenOCD \cite{openocd} is the debug server in general use for ARM microcontrollers, and it reaches the debug port, the access ports and the memory of the target. Its support for trace units covers the architecture implemented in the ARM9 generation of processors, and its manual describes that support as little used and possibly unreliable. It offers no driver for the on-chip buffer of the devices considered here.

pyOCD \cite{pyocd} reaches the debug port over the same class of probe and exposes instrumentation trace only. Instrumentation trace answers a different question. It reports what the program was written to announce, which is distinct from the instructions the processor executed.

The decoder itself exists. OpenCSD \cite{opencsd} implements the trace protocols of the current architecture and is the decoder used by the Linux kernel profiling infrastructure to interpret captures taken on application-profile processors \cite{perf_cs_etm}. That arrangement presumes a host running on the traced device, kernel drivers configuring the trace components from within it, and a processor of the application profile. On a bare-metal microcontroller the host is external and reaches the device only through the debug port, and none of the surrounding infrastructure transfers. The decoder is available and what stands on either side of it is not.

\subsection{Published Work}
\label{subsec:sota_academic}

Instruction trace has been applied in published work to problems adjacent to debugging. FrankenTrace \cite{frankentrace} concerns execution tracing on Cortex-M systems-on-chip, and Ninja \cite{ninja} concerns tracing and debugging on ARM processors. Both establish that the hardware supports uses beyond the ones the vendor environments expose.

\section{Objectives, Constraints and Deliverables}
\label{sec:objectives}

The objective follows from the question of Section \ref{sec:problem}. The trace hardware is configured over the ordinary debug connection, the capture is retrieved from the on-chip buffer and reconstructed against the program image, and the executed instructions are presented in source terms inside a development tool during a live debug session, the reconstruction remaining usable on a capture taken independently of any session.

The first constraint carries a consequence worth stating here. A buffer inside the device holds a bounded quantity of trace. A capture is therefore a window of the most recent execution, and its extent is fixed by the memory the device can spare. Section \ref{sec:results} states the quantity of trace measured for a capture taken on the target.

\subsection{Deliverables}

The work is delivered in three parts. An application note gathers into one document the procedure for using instruction trace on these devices. A proof of concept demonstrates that a capture is obtained from an on-chip sink, decoded against the program image, and reconstructed into the executed instructions. A debugger carries that capability into a development tool, alongside breakpoints, memory and variable inspection, and disassembly.

Two of these rest on components adopted from existing projects. The debug server and the debug engine are existing open-source projects, and the decoding of the trace protocol is performed by an existing library. Written for this work are the drivers that configure the trace components and retrieve the capture, the chain that reconstructs the executed instructions from it, the components that constitute the core of the debug engine, and the extension that presents the result. Chapter \ref{ch:implementation} states that division component by component.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter has established that the ordering of a program's execution is required to locate an important class of defect, and that neither halting the processor nor instrumenting the program supplies it. It stated the question the project answers, namely how the instruction trace capability of STM32 devices is made reachable in an open and integrated debug chain without a dedicated trace probe, and divided that question into configuration, capture, interpretation and restitution. The survey established that the hardware provides the capability, that the proprietary environments expose it only through their own probes, and that the open tools reach a decoder and no capture. The objective, the constraints and the three deliverables were stated. The chapter that follows establishes the concepts on which the answer rests.
